# Main empirical findings of quantitative cross-country studies on well-being, health, public spending, democracy and development

Notes on sourcing. Items marked **[web]** were confirmed through searches in this session (Oct 2026). Items marked **[lit]** are standard, widely cited results from the peer-reviewed literature. They are cited to the original paper's canonical URL or DOI but were **not re-fetched** in this session. The report writer should treat [lit] effect sizes as accurate to the best of my knowledge and worth spot-checking before they are quoted verbatim. Three hosts could not be fetched in this session because of network restrictions: gallup.com, v-dem.net and worldhappiness.report. Figures from them come from press coverage of the primary documents.

Labels used throughout: **CONSENSUS** = broadly replicated. **CONTESTED** = active disagreement in the literature. **CORR** = correlational evidence only. **CAUSAL** = quasi-experimental or instrumented identification claimed.

## 1. Happiness / subjective well-being (SWB): income, WHR factors, social capital, youth decline

### Takeaway
Across countries, average life evaluation (Cantril ladder) rises roughly linearly with **log** GDP per capita, with no satiation point (CONSENSUS, CORR). Whether *long-run growth within a country* raises happiness (the Easterlin paradox) is still CONTESTED. The WHR's six factors explain about three-quarters of the cross-country variance in national averages, which is the right benchmark for the user's R² ≈ 0.74. Social factors (support, trust, freedom, low corruption) carry large weight beyond income. Since 2024 the WHR has documented falling youth well-being that is concentrated in English-speaking countries and Western Europe.

### Cited Findings
**Income and happiness**
- Easterlin (1974) found that within countries richer people are happier, but over time national happiness did not rise with income. Easterlin et al. (2010) reported no significant long-run (≥10 yr) relationship between GDP growth and life-satisfaction trends across 37 countries. CONTESTED. — [lit] [Easterlin et al. 2010, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1015962107)
- Stevenson & Wolfers (2008) found that the life-satisfaction/log-income gradient is similar within countries, across countries and over time, and saw no evidence of satiation, which challenges the paradox. — [lit] [Stevenson & Wolfers 2008, Brookings Papers](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/economic-growth-and-subjective-well-being-reassessing-the-easterlin-paradox/)
- Deaton (2008), using Gallup World Poll data, found life satisfaction linear in log GDP per capita. A given *proportional* income increase has a similar effect in rich and poor countries, so in levels the effect per dollar is much smaller in rich countries. — [lit] [Deaton 2008, J. Econ. Perspectives](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.22.2.53)
- Kahneman & Deaton (2010) found that life evaluation keeps rising with log income, while day-to-day *emotional* well-being plateaued around US$75,000/yr (US data, binary yesterday-emotion items). — [lit] [Kahneman & Deaton 2010, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1011492107)
- Killingsworth (2021), using experience-sampling data on a continuous scale, found experienced well-being rising linearly with log income with no plateau. The 2023 adversarial collaboration (Killingsworth, Kahneman, with Mellers as arbiter) reanalysed the data. The flattening appeared **only among the least happy ~20%**, and happiness kept rising with log income for the majority. Their conclusion was that "both were partly right". — [web] [Princeton/Penn release](https://behavioralpolicy.princeton.edu/news/DK_wellbeing0323); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2023/03/230306163006.htm). A secondary blog cites "15%", but the primary release says 20%.
- Gelman cautions that these are average associations from survey data and should not be read deterministically or causally. — [web] [Statistical Modeling blog](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2023/08/01/studying-average-associations-between-income-and-survey-responses-on-happiness-be-careful-about-deterministic-and-causal-interpretations-that-are-not-supported-by-these-data/)

**WHR explanatory model**
- The WHR explains national ladder averages with six variables: log GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption. — [web] [WHR 2019 ch. 2 (via link)](https://worldhappiness.report/ed/2019/changing-world-happiness/); [WHR FAQ mirror](https://tampere2020.iamcr.org/worldhappiness.report/faq/index.html)
- Together these explain roughly **three-quarters** of the cross-country variance in national averages, whereas individual-level R² is only about 0.25. — [web, secondary] [noregress Substack summary](https://noregress.substack.com/p/how-to-choose-happiness-part-6-what). The WHR chapter-2 text in recent editions says the six variables explain "more than three-quarters" of the variation [lit], but I could not fetch it for verification.
- The WHR itself warns that the variables are inter-correlated and that causation may run both ways, for example from health to happiness and from happiness to health. — [web] [WHR FAQ mirror](https://tampere2020.iamcr.org/worldhappiness.report/faq/index.html)
- In WHR decompositions, GDP per capita, social support and healthy life expectancy usually contribute the largest absolute shares of the gap with "Dystopia", followed by freedom. Generosity and corruption contribute least on average, though corruption matters a lot in some regions. — [lit] [WHR 2023 ch. 2](https://worldhappiness.report/ed/2023/world-happiness-trust-and-social-connections-in-times-of-crisis/)

**Trust, social support, benevolence**
- Helliwell and colleagues' work on social trust (including trust in finding a lost wallet) shows that high-trust settings have higher SWB. Trust also buffers the well-being losses from unemployment, ill health and discrimination. — [lit] [Helliwell, Huang & Wang 2016, "New evidence on trust and well-being", NBER w22450](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22450)
- The WHR 2025 theme was "caring and sharing". Costa Rica was 6th (score 7.274) and Mexico 10th (6.979), the first time Latin American countries entered the top 10. Argentina was 42nd, its best since 2019. Finland was 1st (7.736). — [web] [Time Out list](https://www.timeout.com/news/world-happiness-report-the-full-list-of-countries-for-2025-032025); [OSA Tropical](https://osatropicalproperties.com/blog/2025-world-happiness-report-focus-on-costa-rica); [Derecha Diario](https://derechadiario.com.ar/us/argentina/argentina-grew-in-the-happiness-ranking-and-reached-its-best-position-since-2019)

**WHR 2026 (published 19 Mar 2026; 3-yr averages 2023-2025)**
- Finland was 1st for the 9th straight year, followed by Iceland, Denmark, Sweden and Norway. Costa Rica was **4th** (≈7.44), the highest-ever Latin American position. Mexico was 12th (6.972), the US 23rd (6.816; the US was 24th in 2025, its lowest ever), Brazil 32nd, Spain 41st, **Argentina 44th**, Venezuela 80th. — [web] [Fox (syndicated)](https://www.fox5ny.com/news/worlds-happiest-countries-2026-revealed-where-us-ranks); [UnoTV](https://www.unotv.com/nacional/mexico-ocupa-el-lugar-12-en-el-ranking-de-la-felicidad-2026-finlandia-islandia-y-dinamarca-estan-a-la-cabeza/); [Mappr](https://www.mappr.co/?p=52335)
- The WHR 2026 theme was social media and youth. Heavy users (several hours/day) report lower happiness, with the strongest negative association among **young women**. Users of under 1 hour/day report the *highest* well-being, higher than non-users. The steepest youth declines are in the US, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, plus parts of Western Europe. In Latin America and the Middle East youth well-being has not fallen despite heavy use, and the associations there are more positive. The report says it **cannot establish causality**. — [web] [Oxford Wellbeing Research Centre](https://www.ox.ac.uk/news/2026-03-19-world-happiness-report-2026-shows-complex-global-picture-social-media-and-happiness); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-worlds-happiest-countries-report-calls-attention-to-youth-well-being/)
- The WHR 2024 found that in North America (and ANZ) the young (<30) are now less happy than the old. For the under-30s alone, the US ranked 62nd, and the US dropped out of the overall top 20. — [lit] [WHR 2024](https://worldhappiness.report/ed/2024/)

**Latin American "happiness premium"**
- Latin American countries consistently score higher on life evaluations and positive affect than the six-factor model predicts (positive residuals). Rojas attributes this to warm family and social relations. — [lit] [Rojas, WHR 2018 ch. 6, "Happiness in Latin America has social foundations"](https://worldhappiness.report/ed/2018/happiness-in-latin-america-has-social-foundations/)

### Inferences
- The user's R² ≈ 0.74 (n=119, 2021) is right at the WHR benchmark of "about three-quarters". This is expected rather than impressive, because cross-sectional national averages are dominated by the income–health–institutions gradient. The user's model swaps in corruption control (likely WGI rather than Gallup perceptions), health spending and Gini in place of social support, freedom and generosity, and reaches similar fit. That points to shared variance (a development "syndrome"), so coefficients on individual covariates should not be read causally.
- Expect strong multicollinearity between log GDP, life expectancy, corruption control and health spending. Coefficients may be unstable or wrongly signed. VIFs and partial R² are recommended.
- Latin American countries (including Argentina, but more so Costa Rica and Mexico) will likely show positive residuals. A regional dummy or a social-support variable would test the "Latin premium".
- 2021 is a COVID-affected year. The WHR reported a rise in benevolence (helping strangers, donating) in 2020-2022, so single-year cross-sections may differ from 2019 or 2023.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch the WHR 2025/2026 chapter-2 tables (worldhappiness.report and gallup.com were blocked), so exact variance shares and coefficients for each of the six factors in the latest editions are unverified.
- The WHR 2025 wallet-return findings (expected vs actual return rates) were not confirmed in this session.

## 2. Health: Preston curve, health spending vs outcomes, US outlier, COVID-19 shock and recovery

### Takeaway
Life expectancy rises steeply with income at low incomes and flattens at high incomes (the Preston curve: concave, roughly linear in log income). The curve has shifted upward over time, so most historical gains came from technology and public health rather than income growth (CONSENSUS, CORR). Cross-national effects of health *spending* on outcomes are positive but small and diminishing once income is controlled for. The US is the canonical high-spending, low-outcome outlier. COVID-19 cut global life expectancy by about 1.8 years (2019–2021), and it had essentially recovered by 2023.

### Cited Findings
- Preston (1975) showed a concave cross-country relation between life expectancy and national income that shifted upward between the 1930s and 1960s. He attributed most of the life-expectancy gains (roughly 75–90%) to factors other than income growth. — [lit] [Preston 1975, Population Studies](https://doi.org/10.1080/00324728.1975.10410201)
- Pritchett & Summers (1996) use instrumental variables to argue that income causally improves health ("wealthier is healthier"). The long-run income elasticity of infant mortality is about −0.2 to −0.4 (CAUSAL claim, IV). — [lit] [Pritchett & Summers 1996, J. Human Resources](https://doi.org/10.2307/146062)
- Filmer & Pritchett (1999) found that public health spending explains very little cross-national variation in child mortality. Income, inequality, female education, ethnic fractionalization and religion explain about 95%. The estimated cost per child death averted through public spending is very high. — [lit] [Filmer & Pritchett 1999, Soc Sci Med](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0277-9536(99)00149-4)
- Anand & Ravallion (1993) found that income's association with life expectancy works mostly through poverty reduction and public health spending. Once those are controlled for, GDP per capita has little independent effect. — [lit] [Anand & Ravallion 1993, J. Econ. Perspectives](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.7.1.133)
- Efficiency frontiers (DEA/SFA) show large dispersion: many countries could reach the same outcomes with less spending, or better outcomes with the same spending. — [lit] [Evans, Tandon, Murray & Lauer 2001, BMJ](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.323.7308.307); [Herrera & Pang 2005, IMF WP/05/151](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2005/wp05151.pdf)
- The US spends about 17% of GDP on health, far above other rich countries, yet has lower life expectancy and ranks last overall among high-income peers in the Commonwealth Fund comparisons. — [lit] [Commonwealth Fund, Mirror, Mirror 2024](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/fund-reports/2024/sep/mirror-mirror-2024); [OWID, health spending vs life expectancy](https://ourworldindata.org/the-link-between-life-expectancy-and-health-spending-us-focus)
- US "deaths of despair" (drug overdoses, suicide, alcohol) among middle-aged non-Hispanic whites without degrees reversed mortality declines from 1999. — [lit] [Case & Deaton 2015, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1518393112)
- **COVID shock:** Between 2019 and 2021 global life expectancy fell by **1.8 years**, the largest drop in recent history (WHO World Health Statistics 2025). — [web] [WHO news 15 May 2025](https://www.who.int/news/item/15-05-2025-who-warns-of-slowing-global-health-gains-in-new-statistics-report)
- **Recovery:** According to GBD 2023 (Lancet, Oct 2025), global life expectancy returned to pre-pandemic levels in 2023: 76.3 years for females and 71.5 for males. 194 of 204 locations had at least partial recovery in age-standardised mortality, and about 62% were back to or below 2019 levels. — [web] [GBD 2023, Lancet (Southampton eprint)](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/507322/1/1-s2.0-S0140673625013303-main.pdf); [PubMed 41092927](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41092927/)
- WHO Global Health Estimates (released Oct 2026) put life expectancy at 73.3 years in 2023 against 73.4 in 2019, so nearly recovered. Healthy life expectancy was 62.8 in 2023, still 0.4 years below 2019. — [web] [UN News Oct 2026](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/10/1168515); [CTV](https://www.ctvnews.ca/health/article/life-expectancy-almost-back-to-pre-covid-levels-says-who/)

### Inferences
- In the user's happiness model, life expectancy and log GDP are both on the Preston curve and are highly collinear. Life expectancy should be entered *with* log GDP, and the incremental R² checked.
- When modelling infant mortality or life expectancy on health spending as a share of GDP, expect a weak or insignificant coefficient after controlling for log GDP per capita, education and inequality. This is consistent with Filmer–Pritchett and is not evidence that spending is useless, since spending is endogenous (sicker countries spend more) and composition matters. Spending per capita (PPP) usually correlates more strongly than spending as a share of GDP.
- Panels covering 2020–2022 must handle the COVID dip. A year dummy, or excluding 2020–21 for trend estimates, is advisable. Recovery by 2023 means 2023–2024 values are comparable to 2019.
- Hospital beds and physicians per 1,000 are input measures. Their relationship to outcomes is weak at high levels, and bed counts fall in rich countries as care shifts to outpatient settings.

### Gaps
- Exact US 2024 life expectancy (NCHS) and Argentina-specific post-COVID recovery figures were not verified in this session.

## 3. Suicide: cross-national correlates, global trends 2000–2021, data quality

### Takeaway
The global age-standardised suicide rate fell by about 35% between 2000 and 2021, and the **Americas are the only WHO region where it rose (+17%)**. That rise matters for the Latin American and Argentine data. Male rates are more than twice female rates. Cross-nationally, suicide correlates with alcohol use, unemployment and recessions, and (paradoxically) with high average happiness in rich Western settings (CORR). Suicide data are among the least reliable cause-of-death statistics. Only 86 of 183 countries have good-quality vital registration, and underreporting is concentrated in LMICs.

### Cited Findings
- WHO estimated **727,000 suicides worldwide in 2021** (about 1 in 100 deaths). The global age-standardised rate fell from 9.0 (2019) to 8.9 per 100,000 (2021), and fell **35% from 2000 to 2021**. Declines ranged from 3% in Africa to 50% in the Western Pacific, while the **Americas rose 17%**. WHO projects only about a 12% reduction by 2030, against the SDG target of one-third. — [web] [IASP summary of WHO data, June 2025](https://www.iasp.info/2025/06/02/who-suicide-data/); [Arab News (AFP)](https://www.arabnews.pk/world/suicide-behind-one-in-every-100-deaths-globally-who-2613867); [HumanProgress](https://humanprogress.org/global-suicide-rate-dropped-significantly-between-2000-and-2021/)
- Male vs female global age-standardised rates in 2021 were 12.3 vs 5.6 per 100,000, a ratio of about 2.2. — [web, secondary] [IASP](https://www.iasp.info/2025/06/02/who-suicide-data/)
- Data quality: for the 2021 estimates, 86 of 183 member states had good-quality (high or medium) vital registration data. The 49 HICs with good data cover 99.6% of estimated HIC suicides, but the 37 LMICs with good data cover only 16.3% of LMIC suicides. WHO says underreporting and misclassification (as undetermined intent, accidents or poisonings) are likely worse for suicide than for other causes, partly because of stigma and illegality, and it advises caution with international comparisons. — [web] [WHO suicide data page](https://www.who.int/teams/mental-health-and-substance-use/data-research/suicide-data)
- A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis (71 countries, 122 years) estimated global suicide underreporting at **17.9%**: about 34.9% in LMICs with incomplete registration (for example India and China) and about 11.5% in HICs. Correcting for this implies more than 1 million suicides per year. This is a single-study estimate, not an official revision. — [web] [PMC12378953](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12378953/); see also [Evidence for underregistration of suicide, PMC7676976](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7676976/)
- **Happiness–suicide paradox:** Daly, Oswald, Wilson & Wu found that happier places (US states and Western nations) tend to have higher suicide rates even after adjusting for composition. Their proposed mechanism is "dark contrasts": unhappiness is harder to bear among happier others. For example, Utah ranked 1st in life satisfaction and 9th in suicide, while New York ranked 45th and had the lowest suicide rate. The authors say the cross-country evidence is only suggestive because of cultural and reporting differences. — [web] [FRBSF WP 2010-30](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/working-papers/2010/02/the-happiness-suicide-paradox); [JEBO 2011 (Warwick repository)](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/44742/); [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/happiest-places-have-highest-suicide-rates-says-new-research)
- **Unemployment:** Across 63 countries (2000–2011), unemployment was associated with a 20–30% higher suicide risk, and about 1 in 5 suicides (about 45,000/yr) were attributable to unemployment. — [lit] [Nordt et al. 2015, Lancet Psychiatry](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(14)00118-7)
- **Recessions:** In Europe, each 1-percentage-point rise in unemployment was associated with a 0.79% rise in suicides under 65. Active labour-market spending attenuated the association. — [lit] [Stuckler et al. 2009, Lancet](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(09)61124-7). The 2008 crisis was associated with about 5,000 excess suicides in 2009 in Europe and the Americas. — [lit] [Chang et al. 2013, BMJ](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.f5239)
- **Income:** WHO reports that most suicides (about 73% in 2019–2021) occur in low- and middle-income countries, but *rates* do not follow a simple monotonic relationship with GDP. Several high-income countries (Korea, Lithuania) and some low-income ones have high rates. — [lit] [WHO Suicide fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/suicide)
- **Alcohol:** Per-capita alcohol consumption, especially spirits and binge patterns, is associated with male suicide in time-series studies (for example Russia and Eastern Europe). — [lit] [Norström & Rossow 2016, Suicide Life Threat Behav](https://doi.org/10.1111/sltb.12170)

### Inferences
- The user's WHO crude rates by sex and age are not age-standardised. Cross-country comparisons of crude rates are confounded by age structure: older populations have higher crude rates. Age-standardised rates should be used for between-country comparisons. Crude rates can be used for within-country trends, with caution.
- The rise in the Americas (+17%) contrasts with global declines. Argentina, Uruguay and US trends should be examined against this regional background.
- A positive bivariate correlation between national happiness and suicide in the panel would be consistent with Daly et al. Among rich countries, however, it is more likely to reflect Nordic and Eastern European latitude and culture, alcohol, and reporting quality. It should not be read as causal.
- Data-quality flags (WHO quality tiers) should be included as controls or used to restrict samples. Sub-Saharan African and South Asian values are largely modelled estimates.

### Gaps
- Argentina-specific suicide trends and WHO quality rating were not retrieved.
- I found no recent (2023–2026) multi-country meta-regression giving pooled elasticities of suicide with respect to GDP per capita.

## 4. Public spending: education spending vs learning, Wagner's law, redistribution, efficiency

### Takeaway
Cognitive skills (test scores), not years of schooling or spending, predict long-run growth (Hanushek & Woessmann; causal claim CONTESTED but influential). Cross-country correlations between education spending and test scores are weak once income is controlled for. However, quasi-experimental within-country evidence (US) finds that money does matter when spent well. Government size rises with income (Wagner's law; supported in many but not all tests). Taxes and transfers reduce inequality substantially in OECD countries but much less in Latin America. Spending-efficiency frontiers show wide dispersion.

### Cited Findings
- Hanushek & Kim (1995): a 1 SD difference in measured cognitive skills was associated with about **1 percentage point** higher annual growth, much more than years of schooling. They noted that simple resource-based approaches are generally ineffective at raising skills. — [web] [NBER w5399](https://www.nber.org/papers/w5399); [McMaster abstract](https://www.ciln.mcmaster.ca/abstracts/hanushek.htm)
- Hanushek & Woessmann (2008 JEL; 2015 *Knowledge Capital of Nations*) put the effect of a 1 SD test-score difference at about **2 percentage points** of annual growth over 40 years. They argue for causality using institutional instruments and the finding that countries that improved skills grew faster. — [web, causal argument] [NBER w14633](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14633.pdf); [lit, 2 pp figure] [Hanushek & Woessmann 2008, JEL](https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.46.3.607). The 2 pp figure was not confirmed by search, but the 2009 paper's causal strategy was.
- Hanushek (2003) found that cross-country and US production-function studies show no consistent relationship between resources (spending, class size) and achievement. — [lit] [Hanushek 2003, Economic Journal](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0297.00099). This is CONTESTED by Jackson, Johnson & Persico (2016), where a 10% increase in per-pupil spending over 12 school years raised educational attainment by 0.31 years and adult wages by about 7%, with larger effects for low-income children (CAUSAL, US school finance reforms). — [lit] [JJP 2016, QJE](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjv036). A meta-analysis found that $1,000 more per pupil for 4 years raises test scores by about 0.03 SD and college-going by about 2.8 pp. — [lit] [Jackson & Mackevicius 2024, AEJ: Applied](https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20210863)
- **Wagner's law** (public spending grows faster than income): empirical tests are mixed. Support is stronger in developing and long-run historical data and weaker or reversed in some advanced economies. — [lit] [Peacock & Scott 2000, "The Curious Attraction of Wagner's Law", Public Choice](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1005292229211); [Durevall & Henrekson 2011, J. Public Econ.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2011.01.003)
- **Redistribution:** in the OECD, taxes and transfers reduce the Gini from market to disposable income by about a quarter on average, with most of the effect coming from transfers (especially pensions). In Latin America the fiscal reduction of inequality is much smaller (often 2–5 Gini points), with Argentina and Brazil among the larger reducers in the region. — [lit] [OECD Income Distribution Database](https://www.oecd.org/social/income-distribution-database.htm); [Lustig (ed.) 2018, CEQ Handbook](https://commitmentoequity.org/publications-ceq-handbook/)
- **Efficiency:** DEA and stochastic-frontier studies of education and health spending find large input inefficiencies. The same outcomes are typically attainable with 20–40% less spending in the median developing country (Gupta & Verhoeven for Africa; Herrera & Pang). — [lit] [Gupta & Verhoeven 2001, J. Policy Modeling](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0161-8938(01)00052-4); [Herrera & Pang 2005, IMF](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2005/wp05151.pdf)

### Inferences
- In the user's democracy logit, education spending as a share of GDP is a weak proxy for human capital. Attainment (Barro-Lee years of schooling) or test scores would be closer to the theory (Lipset, Glaeser et al.), and spending is partly endogenous to democracy, since democracies spend more on education.
- A positive association between health spending and happiness most likely reflects income and institutional quality. As in the literature, the user should expect it to shrink when log GDP and governance are included.
- Spending as a share of GDP has a mechanical denominator problem: GDP falls in a recession and the ratio rises. Using per-capita PPP spending or lags is advisable.

### Gaps
- No single recent meta-analysis of *cross-country* education spending and PISA effect sizes was retrieved. The OECD's "spending above about USD 50,000 cumulative per student is unrelated to PISA" finding is widely cited [lit, OECD PISA 2018 Vol. I/V], but its exact threshold was not verified here.

## 5. Democracy and development: modernization, income → democracy, democracy → growth, backsliding, resource curse

### Takeaway
The cross-sectional correlation between income and democracy is among the strongest in comparative politics (Lipset; Barro). Whether income *causes* democratization is CONTESTED. It disappears with country fixed effects (Acemoglu et al. 2008), while Boix–Stokes and later work find endogenous effects. Income clearly stabilizes existing democracies (Przeworski et al.). Democracy causes about 20% higher GDP per capita over 25 years (Acemoglu et al. 2019, CAUSAL claim, debated magnitude). Since about 2010, autocratization has outpaced democratization, and V-Dem's 2026 report records 44 countries autocratizing, including the US. Oil wealth is associated with autocracy (Ross), though this is contested in panel data.

### Cited Findings
- Lipset (1959): "the more well-to-do a nation, the greater the chances that it will sustain democracy." — [lit] [Lipset 1959, APSR](https://doi.org/10.2307/1951731). Barro (1999) confirmed that income, primary schooling and a smaller urban–rural education gap predict democracy, and that oil and Islam-majority status were negatively associated with it. — [lit] [Barro 1999, JPE](https://doi.org/10.1086/250107)
- Przeworski & Limongi (1997) and Przeworski et al. (2000): income does not trigger transitions (no "endogenous" democratization), but democracies above about US$6,000 (1985 PPP, Argentina 1976's level) never broke down ("exogenous" survival). — [lit] [Przeworski & Limongi 1997, World Politics](https://doi.org/10.1353/wp.1997.0004)
- Boix & Stokes (2003) find endogenous democratization when longer historical samples (from 1850) are used. — [lit] [Boix & Stokes 2003, World Politics](https://doi.org/10.1353/wp.2003.0019)
- Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson & Yared (2008): with country fixed effects and IV, the effect of income on democracy disappears. Historical, institutional factors drive both. — [lit] [AJRY 2008, AER](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.98.3.808). CONTESTED by Treisman (2015, AJPS) and others, who find medium-run income effects around leadership turnover. — [lit] [Treisman 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12135)
- Acemoglu, Naidu, Restrepo & Robinson (2019, JPE): GDP per capita is about **20% higher 25 years** after a permanent democratization (about 175–184 countries, 1960–2010; dynamic panel/IV with regional democratization waves). The effect works through investment, schooling and health, does not depend on the initial level of development, and gains appear only after 10–15 years. Critics call the magnitude modest and note that reverse causality dominates the raw cross-sectional gap. — [web] [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/124287/700936.pdf); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/03/190307131417.htm); [Marginal Revolution critique](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2019/07/is-democracy-doomed.html)
- Education and democracy: Glaeser, Ponzetto & Shleifer (2007) argue that schooling raises civic participation, which favours democracy over dictatorship. — [lit] [Glaeser et al. 2007, J. Econ. Growth](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10887-007-9015-1)
- Resource curse (politics): oil hinders democracy, more strongly in poorer states. — [lit] [Ross 2001, World Politics](https://doi.org/10.1353/wp.2001.0011). CONTESTED: with country fixed effects and long historical data, Haber & Menaldo (2011) find no negative effect. — [lit] [Haber & Menaldo 2011, APSR](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055411000207). For growth, Sachs & Warner found resource abundance associated with slower growth [lit] ([Sachs & Warner 2001, EER](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0014-2921(01)00125-8)), which Brunnschweiler & Bulte (2008) contest by distinguishing abundance from dependence. — [lit] [JEEM 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeem.2007.08.004)
- **V-Dem Democracy Report 2026 ("Unraveling the Democratic Era?")**: **44** countries autocratizing (up from 42), a record number at once. One tally gives 92 autocracies vs 87 democracies at end-2025. The level of democracy for the average world citizen is back to **1978**. The US was downgraded from liberal to electoral democracy, its LDI fell about 24% and its rank dropped from 20th to 51st of 179. Seven EU member states plus the UK and US are autocratizing. Media censorship is present in 32 of the 44 autocratizers and civil-society repression in 30. — [web] [Univ. of Gothenburg press release](https://www.gu.se/en/news/democratic-backsliding-reaches-western-democracies-with-us-decline-unprecedented); [Democracy Without Borders](https://www.democracywithoutborders.org/41651/v-dem-report-finds-global-democracy-is-back-at-1978-levels/); [bne IntelliNews](https://www.intellinews.com/democratic-decline-deepens-in-eastern-europe-as-global-slide-to-autocracy-accelerates-432094/)
- V-Dem Democracy Report 2025 ("25 Years of Autocratization – Democracy Trumped?"): autocracies (91) outnumbered democracies (88) for the first time in about 20 years. About 72% of the world population (5.8 bn) lived in autocracies, and the average citizen's level of democracy was back to about 1985. — [lit] [V-Dem DR 2025](https://www.v-dem.net/documents/54/v-dem_dr_2025_lowres_v1.pdf). **Conflict note:** my recollection is that the 2025 report listed 45 autocratizing countries, but 2026 press coverage says "up from 42". This needs checking against the PDFs.

### Inferences
- The user's democracy logit (log GDP + education spending + MENA dummy, AUC 0.85) reproduces the classic cross-sectional Lipset/Barro pattern. The MENA dummy captures the "Arab democracy deficit", which is partly oil (Ross) and partly historical. The AUC benchmark is consistent with income alone being a strong cross-sectional classifier. However, AJRY (2008) imply that this association should largely vanish with country fixed effects in the 2000–2024 panel. The user should present it as predictive and correlational, not as evidence that income causes democracy.
- Adding a fuel-exports or resource-rents share and a lagged democracy term would align the model with the literature. With fixed effects, within-country variation over 25 years is small for most countries.
- For the V-Dem trends in the panel (2000–2024), expect peak democracy around 2010–2012 and decline afterwards, concentrated in Eastern Europe, South Asia and (latterly) the US. Argentina remains an electoral democracy in V-Dem [lit], but whether it appears on the 2026 autocratization watchlist was not verified.

## 6. Inequality: Gini and well-being, Kuznets curve, inequality and health (Spirit Level)

### Takeaway
The link between income inequality (Gini) and average happiness is weak and context-dependent (CONTESTED). It is negative in Europe and in US time series, mediated by trust and perceived fairness, and null or positive in some developing countries. The Kuznets inverted-U has weak support in modern panel data. The income inequality–population health association exists (meta-analytic RR ≈ 1.08 per 0.05 Gini) but is small and contested as causal. The strong Spirit Level claims are sensitive to country selection.

### Cited Findings
- Alesina, Di Tella & MacCulloch (2004): inequality reduces happiness among the poor and the left in Europe, but has little effect in the US, which they attribute to perceived mobility. — [lit] [J. Public Econ.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2003.07.006)
- Oishi, Kesebir & Diener (2011): Americans were less happy in years with higher inequality (1972–2008), and the effect was mediated by perceived fairness and trust and concentrated among lower-income people. — [lit] [Psychological Science](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611417262)
- Meta-analytic evidence on Gini and SWB is mixed. Ngamaba et al. (2018) reported no strong overall association and differences by development level (negative in developed countries, positive in developing ones). — [lit] [Ngamaba, Panagioti & Armitage 2018, Qual Life Res](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-017-1719-x). The exact pooled estimates were not verified here.
- WHR 2016 argued that inequality *of well-being* predicts national life evaluations and trust better than income inequality does. — [lit] [WHR 2016 Update, ch. 2](https://worldhappiness.report/ed/2016/)
- Kuznets (1955) proposed an inverted-U relationship between inequality and development. Panel evidence with country fixed effects generally fails to find it (Deininger & Squire 1998). Piketty documents rising top shares in rich countries since about 1980. — [lit] [Kuznets 1955, AER](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1811581); [Deininger & Squire 1998, J. Dev. Econ.](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0304-3878(98)00099-6)
- Wilkinson & Pickett (2009, *The Spirit Level*): among about 23 rich countries and the US states, inequality correlates with worse health and social outcomes. — [lit] [The Equality Trust](https://equalitytrust.org.uk/resources/the-spirit-level/). Critiques: the results are sensitive to sample choice (for example excluding Singapore or Hong Kong, and adding countries), they are bivariate, and they lack controls. Leigh, Jencks & Smeeding (2009) found that the inequality–health link is weak or absent in within-country panels. — [lit] [Leigh, Jencks & Smeeding, Oxford Handbook of Economic Inequality](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780199606061.013.0015); [Snowdon 2010, *The Spirit Level Delusion*](https://www.spiritleveldelusion.com/)
- Kondo et al. (2009) meta-analysis (multilevel cohort studies, about 60 million people): RR of mortality **1.08 (95% CI 1.06–1.10) per 0.05 increase in Gini**, with effects only above a Gini of about 0.3. The authors note causality is uncertain. — [lit] [Kondo et al. 2009, BMJ](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.b4471)

### Inferences
- In the user's happiness regression the Gini coefficient will likely be small or insignificant once Latin America (high Gini, high happiness) is included. The "Latin premium" pushes the cross-country Gini–happiness correlation toward zero or positive. A Gini × region interaction, or excluding Latin America, would test whether the coefficient is driven by that region.
- The user's Gini values (WDI or SWIID) mix income-based and consumption-based measures across countries. SWIID standardises them and is preferable for panels.

### Gaps
- A recent (2022–2026) authoritative meta-analysis of Gini and life evaluation using Gallup World Poll data was not found in this session.

## 7. Latin America and Argentina: specific findings

### Takeaway
Latin America is the clearest positive outlier in WHR models: happiness is higher than income, corruption and inequality predict, and youth well-being has not fallen as it has in Anglophone countries. Argentina sits mid-table (42nd in 2025, 44th in 2026) below Costa Rica, Mexico, Uruguay, Brazil and Chile. The Americas are the only WHO region with a rising suicide rate since 2000.

### Cited Findings
- WHR 2025 Latin American ranks: Costa Rica 6, Mexico 10, Belize 25, Uruguay 28, Brazil 36, Panama 41, **Argentina 42**, Guatemala 44, Chile 45, Nicaragua 47. — [web] [Bold Latina](https://www.boldlatina.com/are-latin-americans-happier-what-the-happiness-rankings-reveal/); [Derecha Diario](https://derechadiario.com.ar/us/argentina/argentina-grew-in-the-happiness-ranking-and-reached-its-best-position-since-2019)
- WHR 2026 ranks: Costa Rica 4, Mexico 12, Brazil 32, Argentina 44, Venezuela 80. — [web] [Fox (syndicated)](https://www.foxla.com/news/worlds-happiest-countries-2026-revealed-where-us-ranks); [UnoTV](https://www.unotv.com/nacional/mexico-ocupa-el-lugar-12-en-el-ranking-de-la-felicidad-2026-finlandia-islandia-y-dinamarca-estan-a-la-cabeza/)
- WHR 2026: in Latin America and the Middle East youth well-being has not declined despite heavy social-media use. — [web] [Oxford WRC](https://wellbeing.hmc.ox.ac.uk/news/world-happiness-report-2026-complex-global-picture-of-social-media-and-happiness/)
- Latin American happiness has "social foundations" (family warmth, social relations), producing positive residuals relative to the WHR six-factor model. — [lit] [Rojas, WHR 2018](https://worldhappiness.report/ed/2018/happiness-in-latin-america-has-social-foundations/)
- Americas suicide rate +17% (2000–2021), versus −35% globally. — [web] [IASP](https://www.iasp.info/2025/06/02/who-suicide-data/)
- Przeworski's "income threshold" for democratic survival is anchored on Argentina's 1976 breakdown, the richest democracy ever to collapse in his sample. — [lit] [Przeworski & Limongi 1997](https://doi.org/10.1353/wp.1997.0004)

### Inferences
- Argentina's position (relatively high life expectancy, education spending and democracy scores, but macro volatility and high inflation) makes it a useful case for residual analysis. The happiness-model residuals, and the timing of suicide and happiness changes around crises (2001–02, 2018–19, 2023–24), are worth examining.
